# Take-off, Climb, Approach and Landing Technologies and Procedures for a 2040 300-seat Medium-Range Widebody (100% SAF)

Research status: October 2026. British English. Researcher note: direct fetches of eurocontrol.int and boeing.com failed (DNS errors in this environment), so EUROCONTROL and Boeing figures come from search-result extracts of those pages and of ICAO-hosted presentations. The report writer should treat figures marked "claim" as manufacturer or project assertions, and figures marked "measured" as coming from trials or operational data.

Reference mission used for scaling (researcher's own assumption, not sourced): 300-seat widebody, ~6.5 h, 3,000 NM. A rough trip fuel of ~35–45 t is assumed (roughly 5.5–7 t/h for a modern twin widebody). Percentages below are against ~40 t. Under 100% SAF, a kg of fuel saved is still worth the full (higher) SAF price per kg, so the cost value of each kg saved goes up even though the CO2 lifecycle factor changes.

## Key Question 1: Which measures give a measurable fuel or cost saving per flight, and how big is it relative to a ~6.5 h, 3,000 NM mission?

### Takeaway
Fuel savings from terminal-area measures are real but small against a 6.5 h trip. CDO is about 35–46 kg per arrival in EUROCONTROL's estimate (about 0.1% of trip fuel). Reduced landing flap is about 7–10 kg on a single-aisle jet. Taxi towing is about 95 kg per A320neo cycle (claim). Most of the money comes from cost, not fuel: derated take-off cuts engine maintenance (and actually costs a little fuel), BTV and electric brakes cut brake wear and weight, and CDO and steeper approaches cut noise. Ground-assisted take-off (GABRIEL maglev) is the only concept with a potentially large per-flight benefit, from deleting the landing gear, but it has no quantified validated result and is not realistic for 2040.

### Cited Findings

**Take-off: reduced or derated thrust**
- Mechanism: 5% and 10% derates reduce EGT and can raise EGT margin "by as much as 36 degrees". Derates are normally used when take-off weight is below maximum and the runway is long or the temperature is low — [US Patent 9816897 (wireless engine monitoring)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9816897)
- Boeing Flight Operations (2006): full rated take-off thrust saves fuel compared with derated thrust, but raises overall engine maintenance cost. So derate is a cost measure, not a fuel measure — [Boeing / ICAO workshop, Anderson ops (2006)](https://www2023.icao.int/Meetings/EnvironmentalWorkshops/Documents/ICAO-TransportCanada-2006/Anderson_ops.pdf)
- Open Airlines (vendor, secondary, unverified): reduced-thrust take-off "will increase fuel burn; however, engine life is preserved". It also claims roughly 1% less take-off thrust gives about 10% longer engine life — [Open Airlines, 22 fuel efficiency procedures](https://blog.openairlines.com/22-fuel-efficiency-procedures-for-flight-ops)
- Academic modelling (Indonesian thesis, CFM56-7B, not peer-reviewed): a 25% derate was estimated to extend engine life by up to 5,015 flight cycles. The thesis cites ICF SH&E that maintenance is at least 16% of airline operating cost, with about 40% of that on engines. It also claims "fuel savings of up to 173 kg", which conflicts with the Boeing statement above. The figure probably reflects a specific modelling assumption and should be treated as unreliable — [STTA thesis record](https://rama.kemdikbud.go.id/document/detail/oai:eprints.stta.ac.id:2383-306)
- Aviation Partners Boeing (vendor claim): winglet-enabled climb performance typically allows take-off thrust about 3% lower, slowing EGT deterioration and lengthening time between shop visits. This shows that aerodynamic design improvements (L/D at take-off) translate into derate and maintenance benefit — [Aviation Partners Boeing, Reduced Engine Maintenance Costs](https://aviationpartnersboeing.com/winglets_remc.php)
- Industry: EGT margin deterioration is often the main driver of engine removals — [sassofia.com industry article (2024)](https://sassofia.com/?p=34976)

**Take-off: ground-based assisted take-off (GABRIEL, maglev)**
- GABRIEL (EU FP7, project 284884): September 2011 to August 2014, about €3.3 m, 12 partners from 7 EU countries. Concept: replace the landing gear with an airport-based maglev take-off and landing system — [CORDIS project 284884](https://www.cordis.europa.eu/project/id/284884); [TRIMIS entry](https://trimis.ec.europa.eu/project/integrated-ground-and-board-system-support-aircraft-safe-take-and-landing)
- Claimed benefits (projection, not measured): "aircraft weight can be reduced as no undercarriage will be needed, less fuel needs to be carried onboard and engines can be smaller as less thrust is needed" — [CORDIS news: Magnetic take-off and landing for fuel-efficient aircraft](https://www.cordis.europa.eu/article/id/92581-magnetic-takeoff-and-landing-for-fuelefficient-aircraft)
- Results reported on CORDIS: the concept is "promising", the required technologies are "mature", and Inductrack was recommended for further consideration. Only a small-scale test and a cost-benefit analysis were planned. I found no quantified fuel-saving result in public CORDIS pages — [CORDIS project brief](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/rcn/100028/brief/en)
- A related trajectory-modelling paper optimised only the departure trajectory for minimum fuel, and did not examine noise — [Aircraft Engineering and Aerospace Technology (Emerald)](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/AEAT-11-2013-0207/full/html)

**Take-off and approach: advanced high-lift (A350 ADHF, DFS, morphing, droop nose)**
- A350 XWB trailing edge: two Adaptive Dropped Hinge Flaps (ADHF), covered by a droop panel and seven spoilers — [ICAS 2014 paper 0298](https://www.icas.org/ICAS_ARCHIVE/ICAS2014/data/papers/2014_0298_paper)
- Variable camber with the flaps: moving both flaps together a small amount up or down in cruise gives "up to two per cent" drag reduction at high gross weight. Differential flap setting (DFS) between inner and outer flaps shifts the lift centre and saves about half a tonne of wing-box weight through load alleviation (claim, Airbus-linked project paper) — [EU TRIMIS: Advanced High Lift System Architecture with Distributed Electrical Flap Actuation (2012)](https://trimis.ec.europa.eu/system/files/project/documents/20121030_122633_15453_Advanced_High_Lift_System_Architecture_with_Distributed_Electrical_Flap_Actuation.pdf)
- Liebherr equips the A350 with DFS through an active differential gearbox (ADGB) between the inner and outer flap panels, to optimise cruise lift distribution and reduce drag and fuel. DFS is in service, so TRL 9 — [Liebherr newsletter (2018)](https://www.liebherr.com/en/fra/specials/aer-newsletters/electronics/2018-12_part-1/part-1.html)
- Airbus (Reckzeh 2009): the ADHF cruise benefit is an aerodynamic and weight optimum that a fixed wing cannot achieve — [Reckzeh, KATnet II 2009](https://torroja.dmt.upm.es/congresos/katnet09/pdf/Thursday/Session%20Thu%202/reckzeh_2009_KATnet2_high-lift_aerodynamics.pdf)
- Clean Sky GRA morphing flap: aims to replace a double-slotted flap with a single-slotted camber-morphing flap of similar CLmax and stall angle, with less noise and system complexity. A true-scale 4 m span, 0.9 m chord segment was tested, and the TRL was raised in phase 2 (no formal TRL number given) — [Pecora et al., SPIE 10166 (2017)](https://electronicimaging.spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/10166/101660L/Experimental-validation-of-a-true-scale-morphing-flap-for-large/10.1117/12.2259878.full)
- Clean Sky droop nose: a full-span, gapless droop nose was proposed as a low-noise high-lift device. A 1:6 half-wing wind-tunnel test confirmed the CFD, showing high lift and a large stall angle. Fraunhofer LBF built a 1:1 demonstrator and tested it in an icing wind tunnel, with claimed lower noise than a conventional slat — [ICAS 2012 abstract 747](https://icas.org/ICAS_ARCHIVE/ICAS2012/ABSTRACTS/747.HTM); [Fraunhofer LBF droop nose](https://www.lbf.fraunhofer.de/content/dam/lbf/de/documents/veranstaltungen/Fraunhofer%20Droop%20Nose.pdf)
- A JAXA and Kyushu wind-tunnel study (not Clean Sky) found that a morphing flap with spanwise-varying deflection suppressed flap-edge noise and lowered overall noise compared with a single-slotted flap — [MDPI Aerospace 6(1):1](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-4310/6/1/1)

**Approach: continuous descent operations (CDO)**
- EUROCONTROL 2018 ECAC-wide study: flights not already flying CDO from top of descent spent on average 217 s in level flight. Estimated saving 46 kg fuel, 145 kg CO2 and €20 per such flight. Network average 35 kg fuel, 110 kg CO2 and €15 per arrival. Optimised climb and descent together could save up to 340,000 t of fuel per year in Europe (modelled estimate from flight data) — [EUROCONTROL, Continuous climb and descent operations](https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/continuous-climb-and-descent-operations)
- EUROCONTROL via ICAO: ASBU Block 0/1 modules implemented before 2015 save 57–92 kg fuel (180–289 kg CO2) per flight combined. CDO and three other modules make up close to 60% of the upper range — [EUROCONTROL Operational mitigations, ICAO EUR/NAT seminar 2019](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/EURNAT/Documents/EUR%20and%20Nat%20Docs/Environment/2019%20Regional%20Seminar%20on%20Environment/EUROCONTROL-Operational-mitigations_final.pdf)
- Schiphol study (TU Delft repository): average 92 kg, and up to 500 kg, of fuel per flight. The higher figures likely refer to night-time or long CDA profiles and should be checked — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_360708af-c68d-4c5c-8f27-159a277f6e50)
- Zagreb CDA flight trial: about 100 kg per flight for a descent from about 10,000 ft (snippet truncated, verify) — [Promet – Traffic & Transportation](https://traffic.fpz.hr/index.php/PROMTT/article/view/247)

**Approach: steeper approaches**
- A318 steep approach, London City: EASA certified it for a 5.5° slope (against a nominal 3°) in 2007, after compatibility tests at London City in 2006. PW6000 versions were approved first, CFM56 later. Certification needs a 7.5° capability demonstration (5.5° plus a 2° margin). Changes: a "steep approach" cockpit button, spoilers used as airbrakes, and adjusted approach speed and GPWS/TCAS alerting. This followed a four-year Airbus, EASA and airport programme — [FlightGlobal, Heading for the Square Mile](https://www.flightglobal.com/heading-for-the-square-mile/67515.article); [Airbus A318 history](https://airbus.com/en/who-we-are/our-history/commercial-aircraft-history/previous-generation-aircraft/a318); [AIN 2006](https://oldain.ainonline.com/aviation-news/air-transport/2006-11-27/airbus-eyes-a318-steep-landings)
- Heathrow 2015–16 RNAV 3.2° trial (measured): about 1.9% of arrivals took part, with an average SEL reduction of 0.5 dBA and no adverse safety or operational impact — [LADACAN / Luton DCO submission, Slightly Steeper Approach Study 2021](https://infrastructure.planninginspectorate.gov.uk/wp-content/ipc/uploads/projects/TR020001/TR020001-002476-LADACAN-Post-hearing-submission-Slightly-Steeper-Approach-Study-Report-2021.pdf); [Heathrow press release](https://www.heathrow.com/latest-news/heathrow-launches-steeper-approach-trial-to-reduce-noise)
- Geometry: a 3.2° path is about 340 ft/NM against about 318 ft/NM at 3.0°, which is about 170 ft higher at 8 NM — [same Luton submission](https://infrastructure.planninginspectorate.gov.uk/wp-content/ipc/uploads/projects/TR020001/TR020001-002476-LADACAN-Post-hearing-submission-Slightly-Steeper-Approach-Study-Report-2021.pdf)
- Frankfurt and Heathrow 3.2° combined (secondary): average 0.7 dB, maximum 1.4 dB at about 8 NM. Higher approach speeds of large aircraft limit how steep they can go, and 3.2° reduces pilot acceptance in tailwind — [UC Davis arrival noise presentation (J. Bonds)](https://aqrc.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk1671/files/inline-files/Arrival%20Noise%20Jonathan%20Bonds.pdf)
- Frankfurt 2010 announcement of segmented, curved and steeper approaches: about 40% noise reduction projected, combining several measures. This is a pre-trial claim — [The Local (2010)](https://www.thelocal.com/20100907/29657); see also [DLR elib 87424](https://elib.dlr.de/87424/1/0954410013497462.full.pdf)
- 4.5° trials: no primary source found (see Gaps).

**Landing: reduced flap, idle reverse, BTV, electric brakes**
- Reduced landing flap (CONF 3 instead of FULL on an A320): 7–10 kg fuel per flight on a single-aisle jet (vendor estimate). Not usable at high-altitude airports, on short runways or on contaminated runways — [Open Airlines, reduced landing flaps](https://blog.openairlines.com/what-you-need-to-know-about-reduced-landing-flaps)
- Airbus warning: landing in CONF 3 with REV IDLE and autobrake MED/4/HI is not recommended because it may significantly increase carbon brake oxidation. This is a real cost trade-off between fuel economy measures and brake wear — [Airbus Safety First, Smart adoption of fuel economy initiatives (2019)](https://flightsafety.airbus.com/2019/11/22/smart-adoption-of-fuel-economy-initiatives/)
- Idle reverse: no credible per-landing kg figure found. One secondary article claims 5% "per landing" from an unnamed carrier (unverified, treat as unreliable) — [MightyTravels](https://www.mightytravels.com/2024/03/atr-s-reverse-thrust-activation-unveiled-in-unprecedented-a/). A patent notes reverse-idle-only landings lower engine maintenance cost and noise — [US Patent 10703498](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10703498). The industry trend is to minimise reverser use except when full reverse is needed — [CTU thesis, B737 MAX deceleration](https://dspace.cvut.cz/handle/10467/111479?show=full). Open Airlines argues that idle reverse need not raise brake cost — [Open Airlines blog](https://blog.openairlines.com/the-brake-wear-myth-why-idle-reverse-thrust-dont-have-to-cost-you-at-the-gate)
- Airbus Brake-to-Vacate (BTV): the crew selects the exit and BTV brakes the aircraft to reach it at a suitable speed, minimising runway occupancy time (ROT). It is standard on the A380, optional on the A320 family and available on the A350 — [SKYbrary, Brake to Vacate](https://skybrary.aero/articles/brake-vacate-btv); Airbus FAST 44 (Villaumé, June 2009), cited in the same sources
- BTV claimed brake-life extension of up to 25% (Airbus claim, 2009) — [AIN A380 pilot report 2009](https://oldain.ainonline.com/aviation-news/aviation-international-news/2009-06-30/pilot-report-airbus-a380)
- BTV fuel: no Airbus-quantified fuel figure found. The patent literature argues that earlier exit means less fuel burnt in landing and taxi — [US Patent 8515601](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8515601)
- Boeing 787 electric brakes (Safran): weight saving up to 64 kg (141 lb) per 787-8 and 111 kg (244 lb) per 787-9 (manufacturer claim). No hydraulic lines. Motor and actuators are separate LRUs, with four independent actuators per wheel, so the aircraft can dispatch with one inoperative. Continuous carbon-wear measurement is shown in the cockpit. The 2006 claim was reliability "at least as good" as hydraulic. Riyadh Air chose them in November 2025 with weight as a key factor — [Safran 787 electric brake](https://www.safran-group.com/products-services/boeing-787-dreamliner-electric-brake); [Safran brochure](https://www.safran-group.com/download/media/2307); [AIN 2009](https://oldain.ainonline.com/aviation-news/air-transport/2009-11-11/787s-maintenance-team-makes-most-delays); [Safran / Riyadh Air 2025](https://www.safran-group.com/pressroom/riyadh-air-selects-safran-s-wheels-electric-carbon-brakes-its-boeing-787-2025-11-20)
- Regenerative braking (energy recovery to an on-board store): no in-service or certified airliner system found. The 787 brakes are electrically actuated friction brakes, not regenerative (see Gaps).

**Electric taxi and towing: one-line 2026 status**
- TaxiBot / electric taxi (October 2026): the world's first all-electric TaxiBot (Smart Airport Systems) has towed easyJet A320neos to Schiphol's Polderbaan since the first passenger flight on 30 April 2026, with service from May 2026. easyJet claims about 95 kg fuel and 299 kg CO2 saved per cycle, and three more units are due by the end of 2026. Hybrid TaxiBots have towed KLM 737s at Schiphol since 2022. The certified approvals cover narrow-bodies only, and a widebody version is still in development — [Schiphol blog](https://www.schiphol.nl/en/sustainability/blog/electric-taxibot-starts-operating-at-schiphol); [Future Travel Experience, June 2026](https://www.futuretravelexperience.com/2026/06/schiphol-launches-worlds-first-electric-taxibot-to-support-more-sustainable-aircraft-taxiing/)

**Approach speed ≤150 kt CAS at MLW: benchmarks**
- A350-900: about 140 kt Vapp at MLW and about 135 kt at touchdown, citing the Airbus aircraft characteristics document through a forum post (secondary, verify against Airbus AC doc) — [Infinite Flight community, Final approach speeds](https://community.infiniteflight.com/t/final-approach-speeds-for-aircraft/528955)
- 787-9: a sim-community figure of 156 kt is unverified and looks high. A generic 787 flaps-30 VREF of about 130–150 kt has also been quoted. The authoritative source would be Boeing's ARC and approach-speeds document — [Boeing airports FAQ PDF](https://www.boeing.com/content/dam/boeing/boeingdotcom/commercial/airports/faqs/arcandapproachspeeds.pdf); [FlyAway Simulation](https://flyawaysimulation.com/ask/answers/boeing-787-landing-speed/)
- ICAO approach category is set by Vat at MLW: Category C is 121–140 kt and Category D is 141–165 kt — [AVSIM forum thread](https://www.avsim.com/forums/topic/491817-approach-category-and-ref-speed-question/)

### Inferences
- Scale relative to a ~40 t trip (researcher's estimate): CDO at 35–46 kg is about 0.09–0.12%. Reduced flap at 7–10 kg (single-aisle; a widebody perhaps 2–3 times that) is about 0.02–0.07%. A 2% cruise drag reduction from ADHF variable camber, if it applies through a real share of cruise, is of order 0.5–1.5% (several hundred kg). That makes it by far the largest fuel lever in this topic area, but it acts in cruise, not take-off or landing. Electric brakes at about 64–111 kg of OEW save very roughly 0.1–0.2% of trip fuel through the weight effect on a 6.5 h sector, using the common rule of thumb of about 3–4% of added mass burnt per hour (rule of thumb, not sourced).
- Derate gives a negative fuel effect (a small penalty from longer time at low altitude) but a significant maintenance-cost benefit. For a 2040 aircraft, the design lever is generous take-off L/D and CLmax (high-lift system and wing span), which lets operators use deep derates routinely, as the winglet case shows.
- Steeper approaches are mainly a noise measure (0.5–1.4 dB measured at 3.2°). No meaningful fuel saving was evidenced. Above about 3.5–4.5°, the aircraft needs certified drag devices (as the A318 did), which is a design requirement with CS-25 certification effort (steep-approach certification needs a +2° margin demonstration).
- Ground-assisted take-off (GABRIEL) needs airport-wide infrastructure, a gear-less airframe and a new CS-25 certification philosophy (no landing gear, so no diversion to an unequipped airport). Its realism for a 2040 entry-into-service is very low, so it should be excluded from 2040 design requirements.
- The CS-25 ≤150 kt CAS reference approach speed target at MLW is consistent with current-generation widebodies: the A350-900 is about 140 kt, Category C/D boundary. Advanced high-lift (ADHF-type, droop nose) helps meet it, but a high-aspect-ratio wing with a lower wing loading is the primary driver. Morphing and droop-nose concepts are TRL ~4–6 (researcher estimate from full-scale ground and wind-tunnel demonstrators) and plausible but not certain for 2040.
- Under 100% SAF, the absolute fuel savings are unchanged in kg, but their cost value rises with the SAF price premium. The CO2 equivalence depends on the SAF lifecycle factor, not 3.16 kg CO2/kg.

### Gaps
- Fetches of EUROCONTROL and Boeing pages failed, so the CDO figures rely on search-extracted text. ICAO Doc 9931 figures were not retrieved.
- No quantified Boeing AERO or Airbus "Getting to Grips with Fuel Economy" values were retrieved for derate fuel penalty, flap-setting fuel difference or climb-profile (e.g. NADP1 against NADP2) fuel differences.
- No primary source was found for 4.5° approach trials for jet airliners (e.g. DLR or SESAR steep-approach trials) or for Schiphol steeper-approach trials.
- No quantified GABRIEL fuel or weight saving or cost-benefit result was found in public CORDIS pages.
- No credible per-landing kg figure was found for idle reverse, and no Airbus fuel figure for BTV.
- No in-service or certified regenerative braking system on any airliner was found.
- No formal TRL is published for the Clean Sky morphing flap or droop nose.
- Optimised or steeper climb (CCO) per-flight fuel figures were not retrieved separately from the combined EUROCONTROL CCO/CDO network estimate.
- Authoritative 787-9 approach speed at MLW is not confirmed.
- No widebody-specific CDO data was found. The EUROCONTROL figures are network averages across all types, so widebody savings are likely higher.

## Key Question 2: Which belong in the aircraft's design requirements versus airline or airport operations?

### Takeaway
The design requirements should include an advanced high-lift system (ADHF-type variable camber and DFS, possibly a droop nose or morphing flap), steep-approach capability (certified drag devices and avionics, at least 4.5°), BTV-type autobrake, electric brakes, the approach-speed target, and towbar/electric-taxi compatibility. CDO and CCO, derate policy, reduced-flap landing, idle reverse and steeper published glide paths are operational or air-traffic measures. Ground-assisted take-off is an airport-infrastructure concept outside 2040 scope.

### Cited Findings
- A350 ADHF and DFS are physical design features (active differential gearbox, electrically actuated flaps) — [Liebherr](https://www.liebherr.com/en/fra/specials/aer-newsletters/electronics/2018-12_part-1/part-1.html); [EU TRIMIS 2012 paper](https://trimis.ec.europa.eu/system/files/project/documents/20121030_122633_15453_Advanced_High_Lift_System_Architecture_with_Distributed_Electrical_Flap_Actuation.pdf)
- Steep approach needed type-specific modifications on the A318 (spoilers as airbrakes, a cockpit selector, alerting changes) and EASA certification including a +2° margin demonstration. It is a design and certification item — [FlightGlobal](https://www.flightglobal.com/heading-for-the-square-mile/67515.article)
- BTV is an avionics and autobrake function, needing airport database exit data and crew procedure — [SKYbrary](https://skybrary.aero/articles/brake-vacate-btv)
- Electric brakes are a design choice of the landing gear and electrical architecture — [Safran](https://www.safran-group.com/products-services/boeing-787-dreamliner-electric-brake)
- CDO and CCO are ATM and airspace measures that depend on procedure design and controller practice — [EUROCONTROL](https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/continuous-climb-and-descent-operations)
- 3.2° approaches are an airport and ANSP procedure (RNAV) flown by existing aircraft — [Heathrow](https://www.heathrow.com/latest-news/heathrow-launches-steeper-approach-trial-to-reduce-noise)
- Reduced-flap landing and idle reverse are airline SOP choices, constrained by runway and brake wear — [Airbus Safety First](https://flightsafety.airbus.com/2019/11/22/smart-adoption-of-fuel-economy-initiatives/)
- Derate is an airline policy, enabled by aircraft performance — [Boeing / ICAO 2006](https://www2023.icao.int/Meetings/EnvironmentalWorkshops/Documents/ICAO-TransportCanada-2006/Anderson_ops.pdf)
- Maglev assisted take-off and landing needs a new airport system — [CORDIS](https://www.cordis.europa.eu/project/id/284884)
- TaxiBot is airport-ground-handling equipment, certified per aircraft type, with a widebody version still in development — [Schiphol](https://www.schiphol.nl/en/sustainability/blog/electric-taxibot-starts-operating-at-schiphol)

### Inferences
Suggested classification (researcher synthesis), with 2040 realism:

| Measure | Design or operations | Per-flight benefit (evidence) | 2040 realism / TRL |
|---|---|---|---|
| Derated or reduced take-off thrust | Ops (design enables it through take-off L/D) | Maintenance cost down, small fuel penalty | In service, TRL 9 |
| Ground-assisted maglev take-off (GABRIEL) | Airport infrastructure plus radical design | Claimed (gear deletion), not quantified | Very low, exclude |
| ADHF / DFS variable camber | Design | Up to 2% cruise drag at high weight (claim), about 0.5 t wing weight (claim) | TRL 9 (A350) |
| Morphing flap / droop nose | Design | Noise reduction (wind tunnel), lift parity | TRL ~4–6 (est.), possible for 2040 |
| Optimised climb (CCO) | Ops / ATM | Included in the 340 kt/yr Europe estimate | Mature procedure |
| CDO | Ops / ATM (FMS capability is design) | 35–46 kg per arrival (EUROCONTROL modelled) | Mature, implementation-limited |
| Steeper approach 3.2° | Airport / ANSP | 0.5–1.4 dB (measured) | In use or trialled |
| Steep approach ≥4.5–5.5° | Design plus certification | Noise; access to restricted airports | Proven on A318; widebody untested |
| Reduced-flap landing | Ops | 7–10 kg single-aisle (vendor) | In service |
| Idle reverse | Ops | Not reliably quantified | In service |
| BTV | Design (avionics) plus ops | Brake life up to +25% (claim); ROT reduction | TRL 9 (A380/A350) |
| Electric brakes | Design | −64 to −111 kg OEW (claim); maintenance | TRL 9 (787) |
| Regenerative braking | Design | None demonstrated | Low TRL |
| TaxiBot / e-taxi | Airport ops (aircraft compatibility in design) | ~95 kg per A320neo cycle (claim) | Narrow-body in service 2026; widebody in development |

### Gaps
- No EASA CS-25 special-condition or AMC text was retrieved for steep-approach certification for large aeroplanes. The A318 case is the only precedent found.
- Airport infrastructure needs for BTV (airport-mapping database) and for widebody TaxiBot were not quantified.
- Cost figures (purchase or installation) for electric brakes, BTV or morphing high-lift were not found.
