# Propulsion technologies for a 300-seat, 5000 NM twin widebody (EIS 2040, 100% SAF)

Research note on method: the network policy blocked direct page fetches (rolls-royce.com, flightglobal.com and safran-group.com all refused), so the figures below come from search-result extracts of the cited pages. Each figure is attributed to the page that carried it. Where a snippet was cut off or unconfirmed, I say so. Manufacturer claims are labelled **[OEM claim]**; independent or research analysis is labelled **[independent]**.

## Q0. What does each candidate technology offer (claimed gain, TRL, thrust-class fit, trade-offs, SAF)?

### Takeaway
A geared, ducted ultra-high-bypass turbofan of the UltraFan type is the only candidate that has run at the target thrust (85,000 lbf on test in 2023) and been designed for widebody cruise Mach. The open fan promises more ("more than 20%") but is sized at about 30,000 lbf for single-aisle aircraft and cruises at about Mach 0.78–0.80. BLI and hybrid-electric give small or uncertain benefits at this size. Higher-OPR cores with CMCs are evolutionary enablers, not standalone architectures.

### Inferences
- **TRL today** (my assessment from the status above; no source states these):
  - UltraFan 80: about TRL 5–6, full-scale ground demonstrator at full power
  - RISE open fan: about TRL 4–5, PDR passed, sub-scale wind-tunnel and module tests, no full engine run yet
  - Widebody BLI: about TRL 3–4
  - Widebody hybrid-electric: about TRL 2–3; MW-class parts are flying on regional aircraft, but a widebody needs tens of MW
  - CMC/high-OPR: TRL 9, in service on GE9X and LEAP
- **Thrust-class fit for 70,000–80,000 lbf**: UltraFan 80 is a direct match. An open fan scaled from about 30,000 lbf at about 4 m would, if fan area scales with thrust (diameter with √thrust), need a rotor of roughly 6 m at 75,000 lbf. This is my estimate. Under-wing ground clearance would then be impractical, pushing the design towards a rear or high mount, and the airframe would need a cruise Mach around 0.78–0.80 instead of 0.85. That fits a 3000 NM economic mission better than 5000 NM block-time competitiveness.
- **Realism for EIS 2040**: geared UHBR of the UltraFan type is the most realistic in widebody size. However, the 2026 product push for both RR (UltraFan 30) and CFM (RISE) is narrowbody, so a 2040 widebody engine would need launch commitments around 2032–2034, which do not exist today.
- **Certification**: a geared widebody engine raises CS-E issues mainly around power-gearbox integrity and oil/thermal management; this is my inference. An open fan raises CS-E blade-off and blade-release containment issues, plus CS-25 fuselage and blade-release impact zones and cabin noise and vibration. Under EASA and FAA, these are likely to need special conditions; this is my inference, and I found no source on CS-E special conditions for open fans.

## Q1. Which engine architecture gives the largest credible TSFC gain by 2040 for this aircraft size, and how much of the ~29% fuel-energy-per-seat target can propulsion alone deliver?

### Takeaway
At 70,000–80,000 lbf with Mach 0.85 cruise, the geared, ducted UHBR turbofan (UltraFan type) is the most credible architecture. It offers about 10% better efficiency than the Trent XWB **[OEM claim]**, perhaps a few points more with 2040 core and CMC upgrades. The open fan's headline gain is larger (more than 20% against "today's best", i.e. a LEAP-class baseline), but carries big widebody penalties. Propulsion alone is likely to deliver roughly a third to a half of a 29% per-seat target. This depends on the baseline, which the cited claims do not share.

### Cited Findings
- UltraFan: 10% better than the Trent XWB and 25% better than first-generation Trent **[OEM claim]**, demonstrated at 85,000 lbf — [FlightGlobal, 2023](https://www.flightglobal.com/engines/2023/11/rolls-royce-runs-ultrafan-at-full-power/); [Rolls-Royce](https://www.rolls-royce.com/innovation/ultrafan.aspx)
- RISE: more than 20% better than "today's most efficient engines" **[OEM claim]**, sized for single-aisle aircraft at 25,000–35,000 lbf and Mach 0.78–0.80 — [GE Aerospace, 2021](https://geaerospace.com/press-release/other-news-information/ge-aviation-and-safran-launch-advanced-technology); [AirInsight](https://airinsight.com/the-cfm-rise-program/)
- GE9X: about 5% lower TSFC than XWB-97, with OPR 61 — [Wikipedia GE9X](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/GE9X)
- HyTEC hybrid core: 10% less fuel than best-in-class (single-aisle goal) — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/rtmd/nasa-ge-hybrid-electric-flight/)
- STARC-ABL BLI: 7% fuel on the economic mission and 12% on the design mission (single-aisle; independent NASA analysis) — [NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160007674/downloads/20160007674.pdf)
- ICAO fleet trends: even the most aggressive scenario based on the Independent Expert Integrated Review (IEIR, ICAO Doc 10127) falls short of the 2%/year fuel-efficiency aspiration. The lower-bound IEIR scenario gives about 1.5%/year to 2050 — [ICAO Environmental Trends 2025](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/sp-files/environmental-protection/Documents/EnvironmentReport-2010/2025/Envreport2025_9.pdf); [ICAO Doc 10127](https://store.icao.int/en/independent-expert-integrated-technology-goals-assessment-and-review-for-engines-and-aircraft-english-printed)

### Inferences
- **Baselines differ.** The UltraFan "10%" is against the Trent XWB (A350, EIS 2015). The RISE "20%" is against LEAP-class engines (EIS 2016). The baseline of the "29% target" is not stated in my material. If it is against an A330neo or 787 type (Trent 7000/1000), engine-only gains of about 10–15% are plausible by 2040: about 10% from geared UHBR, plus about 2–5% from 2040-standard OPR, CMC and SAF-optimised combustors. This range is my estimate, not a sourced figure. That would be roughly 35–50% of a 29% target.
- **Open fan on a 300-seat widebody:** the gross gain could be 20% or more. However, lower cruise Mach, rear or high mounting with structural weight, cabin-noise treatment and a 5000 NM design range would likely cut the net per-seat gain substantially. No widebody open-fan study quantifying this was found.
- **Hybrid-electric and BLI** add little at this size by 2040. Hybrid-electric is likely limited to more-electric engine functions (electric start, power off-take, transient assist), worth perhaps 1–3%; this range is speculative. Fuselage BLI depends on a new airframe layout.
- **Ruled-out options:** P&W has no widebody GTF, and RR's 2026 product effort is the 30,000 lbf UltraFan 30. A 2040 widebody engine would therefore need either a decision to scale UltraFan back to widebody size or a GE9X/GEnx successor.

### Gaps
- No source pins down the baseline aircraft or engine for the "~29% fuel-energy-per-seat" target, or how ICAO/CAEP or Clean Aviation splits it between airframe and propulsion.
- No independent (non-OEM) peer-reviewed validation of UltraFan's 10% or RISE's 20%.
- The full ICAO Doc 10127 propulsion chapter (TSFC goals by aircraft class) was not accessible.

## Q2. What are the installation penalties that eat into the headline figures?

### Takeaway
Headline figures are uninstalled or engine-level. Larger fans add nacelle drag and weight, worsen ground clearance and add landing-gear and wing weight. Independent studies show these can erode a large part of the SFC benefit. For open fans, the main penalties are cruise Mach, noise and installation location. For BLI, they are fan distortion and noise.

### Cited Findings
- **General UHBR trade-off [independent]**: larger fans for higher bypass add weight, drag and ground-clearance problems, plus heavier landing gear and wing structure. Uninstalled SFC gains "may be partly or wholly lost" to installation effects and nacelle weight — [ScienceDirect review of UHBR installation effects](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042120300920); [ResearchGate, Ultra-Efficient Engine Diameter Study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23879452_Ultra-Efficient_Engine_Diameter_Study)
- **Quantified example [independent]**: for a 2050-EIS advanced three-shaft engine, a 3.26 m fan (BPR 21.4) gave about 3.9% lower SFC than a 2.95 m fan. The weight penalty reduced the block-fuel benefit to 2.5%, so about 36% of the SFC gain was lost (search-result summary; publication details not visible) — [ResearchGate, UHBR nacelle installation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361982427_Analysis_of_installation_aerodynamics_and_comparison_of_optimised_configuration_of_an_ultra-high_bypass_ratio_turbofan_nacelle)
- **NASA UEET (Daggett)**: below a fan pressure ratio of about 1.4, added nacelle weight and drag offset the efficiency gain once installed — [NASA NTRS 20140001089](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140001089/downloads/20140001089.pdf)
- **Over-wing alternative**: an optimised over-wing UHBR nacelle (L/D = 0.35) still had 2% (5.58 counts) more drag than the under-wing baseline — [ScienceDirect, Aerospace Sci. & Tech. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1270963822004308)
- **Low-FPR fans** also face crosswind and inlet-distortion challenges — [GPPS Journal](https://journal.gpps.global/Crosswind-aerodynamics-of-low-pressure-ratio-fans,205782,0,2.html)
- **Open fan**: cruise is limited to about Mach 0.78–0.80 against Mach 0.85 for widebodies. Unshielded blade noise and vibration need managing. The 13 ft rotor at 30,000 lbf implies a very large rotor at widebody thrust — [AirInsight](https://airinsight.com/the-cfm-rise-program/); [GE Aerospace A380 article](https://www.geaerospace.com/news/articles/farnborough-airshow-technology/gaining-altitude-airbus-a380-test-cfms-open-fan); [Simple Flying, A350 cruise](https://simpleflying.com/fastest-airbus-widebody-jet-service/)
- **BLI**: ingested turbulence and distorted inflow raise noise (NOVA) and fan-durability concerns — [MDPI review](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/14/3/1731)
- **Maintenance**: P&W's GTF Advantage promises "up to twice the time on wing" of the current GTF. This implicitly acknowledges the current GTF's durability problems, a risk relevant to any geared widebody engine — [RTX, Jul 2026](https://www.rtx.com/news/news-center/2026/07/22/rtxs-pratt-whitney-gtf-engines-surpass-800-orders-and-commitments-in-2026-ye)

### Inferences
- For UltraFan-class engines, installation and weight could plausibly absorb about a third of the uninstalled SFC gain, by analogy with the 3.9% to 2.5% example. Rolls-Royce's 10% may be an engine-level figure. Its basis (installed or uninstalled, cruise TSFC or block fuel) is not stated in the material I found.
- **Ground clearance**: a 3.5 m-class fan under the wing of a 300-seat twin likely needs a longer main landing gear or a gull or high-wing arrangement, with a weight penalty. This is my inference; no aircraft-specific source was found.
- **Gearbox**: a 64 MW gearbox adds heat rejection (oil cooler drag) and maintenance-cost risk. In-service GTF durability problems are a cautionary precedent.
- **100% SAF**: this is not an installation penalty for fuel burn, since SAF has a slightly higher specific energy. The certification risks are low-aromatic seal compatibility, fuel-gauging and density calibration, and the absence of an ASTM 100% specification. These matter for the engine type certificate and the aircraft's fuel system. Engine-level test results (RR 2023) suggest no performance impact.
