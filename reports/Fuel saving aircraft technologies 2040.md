# Engines and wings close two-thirds of the gap

A geared ultra-high-bypass turbofan and a high-aspect-ratio composite wing with folding tips and active load alleviation are the two technologies that do most of the work for a 300-seat, Mach 0.85, 2040 twin. Rolls-Royce claims a **10% efficiency gain over the Trent XWB** for its UltraFan, which has run at **about 85,000 lbf on 100% SAF**. Studies credit load alleviation, which is what makes a high-aspect-ratio wing worth building, with **3–10% less fuel**. Smaller sourced gains then build on these: hybrid laminar flow (about 1.6–2.5% on real routes for a wing installation), riblets (about 1%, manufacturer estimate), no-bleed systems (about 3% claimed for the 787) and cruise CG control. Multiplied together rather than added, a realistic design package closes roughly **20 of the ~29 percentage points** needed. Airline and ATM measures (free-route airspace, CDO, reduced contingency fuel, possibly wake-energy retrieval) add perhaps **2–4 points**, but they cannot be written into the aircraft specification. That leaves a residual gap of about **5–7 points**. Only the optimistic end of every range, or a relaxation of the Mach 0.85 requirement, closes it. Most of the headline-grabbing concepts conflict with the brief. Open fans, truss-braced wings, natural-laminar main wings and blended wing bodies are optimised for Mach 0.75–0.80, sized for single-aisle aircraft, or blocked by CS-25 evacuation and Code E questions. Take-off and landing technologies mostly save maintenance cost and noise rather than fuel. All gap-closure figures in this report are my estimates built from sourced component figures, and the two are kept separate throughout.

## The 29% target is a multiplicative, baseline-sensitive budget

Before ranking anything, three accounting rules shape the argument. First, the target is a price-parity condition. SAF at $4/US gal against kerosene at $3/US gal means the aircraft must burn about three-quarters of the competitor's fuel per seat just to hold fuel cost per seat constant, which is a 25% cut per gallon. The ~29% figure in the brief is expressed in fuel energy. The extra few points plausibly reflect SAF's slightly different energy content per unit volume, but that is my interpretation, and the derivation belongs to your team's requirements document. Second, savings compound multiplicatively. A 10% engine gain and a 6% wing gain give 1 − (0.90 × 0.94) = 15.4%, not 16%. Each operational percentage also applies to a smaller fuel base once the aircraft is already efficient, a point the operations research makes explicitly. Third, the baseline matters enormously and is not fixed by any source found. Rolls-Royce quotes UltraFan as **25% better than first-generation Trent and 10% better than the Trent XWB** ([Rolls-Royce](https://www.rolls-royce.com/innovation/ultrafan.aspx); [FlightGlobal](https://www.flightglobal.com/engines/2023/11/rolls-royce-runs-ultrafan-at-full-power/)). Against a 2020 A350-900 (Trent XWB-84), the engine gain is therefore about 10%. Against an A330neo or 787 (Trent 7000/1000), it is plausibly 10–15% (estimate). Your SRR document should name the competitor explicitly, because the size of the gap moves by several points depending on which aircraft it is.

To turn percentages into money, take the researchers' working estimate of **roughly 35–40 t trip fuel** for a 2040 300-seat twin on the 3,000 NM, ~6.5 h mission (unsourced estimate). On that basis, 1% of fuel is about 370 kg, about 122 US gal, or **about $490 per sector at $4/gal** (my arithmetic). Because SAF is a third dearer per gallon, every kilogram saved is worth a third more than it would be on kerosene, so the price premium itself strengthens the case for each technology. A design requirement is worth a percentage on every flight for the aircraft's life. An operational measure depends on airlines, airports and ANSPs choosing to use it.

## Geared UHBR is the only widebody-ready propulsion step

Propulsion is the largest single lever, and only one architecture fits the brief. The **UltraFan 80 demonstrator ran to maximum power at Derby in November 2023, reaching about 85,000 lbf against an 80,000 lbf design target**, on 100% SAF in its first test phase ([FlightGlobal](https://www.flightglobal.com/engines/2023/11/rolls-royce-runs-ultrafan-at-full-power/); [Avitrader](https://avitrader.com/2023/11/13/rolls-royce-successfully-runs-ultrafan-technology-demonstrator-to-maximum-power/)). Its power gearbox delivered 87,000 hp (64 MW) on rig test ([RR fact sheet](https://www.rolls-royce.com/~/media/Files/R/Rolls-Royce/documents/others/ultrafan-80-demonstrator-fact%20sheet.pdf)). That thrust class matches a 300-seat twin directly. The problem is commercial, not technical. The engine has no application, a second build is defined for further testing in 2026, and Rolls-Royce's product effort has moved to the 30,000 lbf **UltraFan 30** for narrowbodies, with €64 m of UNIFIED funding and a 2028 ground test ([Airmappr](https://airmappr.com/news/2026-03/rolls-royce-ultrafan-30-narrowbody); [FlightGlobal, Jul 2026](https://www.flightglobal.com/archive/2026/07/ultrafan-advances-on-two-fronts-as-rolls-royce-readies-for-renewed-test-campaign/)). A 2040 widebody engine would therefore need a launch decision in the early 2030s that does not yet exist. Treat it as realistic but programme-dependent (estimated TRL 5–6 today). No independent measurement of the 10% has been published, only targets.

Headline engine figures shrink once the engine is installed. An independent study found that enlarging a fan from 2.95 m to 3.26 m cut SFC by about 3.9%, but the extra weight cut the block-fuel gain to 2.5%, so **roughly a third of the SFC gain was lost to installation** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361982427_Analysis_of_installation_aerodynamics_and_comparison_of_optimised_configuration_of_an_ultra-high_bypass_ratio_turbofan_nacelle)). NASA's earlier work found that below a fan pressure ratio of about 1.4, nacelle weight and drag offset the gain entirely ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140001089/downloads/20140001089.pdf)). A review of installation effects warns that uninstalled gains "may be partly or wholly lost" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376042120300920)). For your aircraft, a ~3.5 m fan under a low wing implies a longer main gear or a gull wing, with a weight penalty. The P&W GTF's durability troubles are also a warning about geared-engine maintenance cost: the GTF Advantage's promise of "up to twice the time on wing" implicitly concedes them ([RTX](https://www.rtx.com/news/news-center/2026/07/22/rtxs-pratt-whitney-gtf-engines-surpass-800-orders-and-commitments-in-2026-ye)). Higher-OPR cores and CMCs are already TRL 9. GE says CMC parts are **about 25% lighter and need less than half the cooling air** ([GE Reports](https://www.ge.com/news/reports/are-you-ready-for-the-worlds-largest-jet-engine-its-as-wide-as-a-boeing-737-and-super-fuel-efficient)). They are enablers that add a few points to a 2040 core rather than a separate step.

The alternatives do not fit. CFM's RISE open fan targets **more than 20% lower fuel burn than today's best engines**, but it is a ~30,000 lbf single-aisle engine with a ~4 m rotor cruising at about Mach 0.78–0.80 ([GE Aerospace](https://geaerospace.com/press-release/other-news-information/ge-aviation-and-safran-launch-advanced-technology); [AirInsight](https://airinsight.com/the-cfm-rise-program/)). It passed PDR in July 2026, and its A380 flight test has slipped to "by 2030" ([Safran](https://www.safran-group.com/pressroom/open-fan-advances-toward-flight-test-demonstration-decade-2026-07-18)). Scaled to ~75,000 lbf, the rotor would be roughly 6 m across (my estimate). That forces a rear or high mount, and the aircraft could no longer meet Mach 0.85. Boundary-layer ingestion gave **7% fuel on the economic mission and 12% on the design mission** for NASA's single-aisle STARC-ABL ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160007674/downloads/20160007674.pdf)), but no 300-seat study exists and it requires a new airframe layout. Hybrid-electric programmes (HyTEC, EPFD, ELEVATED) are all regional or single-aisle ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/rtmd/nasa-ge-hybrid-electric-flight/); [Aero-Mag](https://www.aero-mag.com/rolls-royce-to-lead-eu-elevated-project-on-hybrid-electric-propulsion)). At widebody scale by 2040, the realistic role is more-electric engine functions worth perhaps 1–3%, which is speculative.

The 100% SAF requirement is mainly a certification issue, not a performance issue. Rolls-Royce tested all its in-production civil engines on 100% SAF with **"no impact on engine performance"** ([Rolls-Royce](https://www.rolls-royce.com/media/press-releases/2023/13-11-2023-poweroftrent-rr-successfully-completes-100-sustainable-aviation-fuel-test-programme.aspx)). However, no ASTM standard yet permits broad use of unblended SAF. D7566 caps most pathways at 50% ([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12757)), partly to keep about 8% aromatics for seal swell ([IEA-AMF](https://iea-amf.org/app/webroot/files/file/Webinar_series_Task66/WS1_Infosheet.pdf)). Seal materials, fuel gauging and density calibration should therefore be design requirements.

## A folding high-aspect-ratio wing beats every radical configuration at Mach 0.85

The largest certifiable airframe gain is a longer, thinner composite wing that folds to fit a Code E gate. The **777X spans 71.8 m in flight and 64.8 m folded**, and Boeing says the tip buys "23 feet (7 m) more span to maximise fuel efficiency" while keeping Code E compatibility ([Boeing via GlobalSpec](https://datasheets.globalspec.com/ds/Boeing/777x/6f06982b-fcf4-4ff5-9453-bb7a9ff28885)). That is a direct in-service precedent for your 65 m limit. Its fuel benefit and fold-mechanism weight have not been published separately. Airbus is maturing the European equivalent. Wing of Tomorrow built three 17 m ground demonstrators with more than 100 manufacturing and assembly technologies. In July 2026 it launched a three-year A321neo flight campaign with full-scale wing extensions that represent a folding wing at full span, targeting a **"double-digit" cut in fuel** (a claim for a single-aisle successor, engines presumably included) ([Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/aerospace/emerging-technologies/airbus-launches-extended-wingspan-a321neo-test-program); [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/aircraft/airbus-wing-of-tomorrow-folding-wings-program/)). The manufacturing work matters for a cost argument as well, because a composite wing can be cheaper to build and not just more efficient. However, no source quantifies the manufacturing cost saving.

A longer wing raises root bending moment and structural mass unless loads are actively controlled ([PoliMi](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/86d9eb8e-e70b-4402-9672-144a8f9a8d2f/CAROG01-25.pdf)). This is why manoeuvre and gust load alleviation belong in the same requirement. A Stanford design study found **10% lower fuel burn and 3.4% lower cost** from load alleviation, rising to about **15% fuel and 5% cost** with natural laminar flow added ([Stanford](https://purl.stanford.edu/hz528zb1688)). DLR found about **3% lower fuel consumption** together with significant wing-box mass reduction, with the optimiser driving towards higher aspect ratio ([DLR](https://elib.dlr.de/137636)). An AIAA paper gives the counterweight: mass savings fall short of the bending-moment reduction once the structure becomes buckling-limited ([ASU AIAA 2016-3744](https://labs.engineering.asu.edu/aircraft-design/wp-content/uploads/sites/115/2023/05/AIAA-2016-3744.pdf)). The defensible reading is that 3–10% is the value of enabling the high-aspect-ratio wing, not an extra benefit on top of it. Against an A350 or 787-class wing, I estimate a net **5–10% per seat** for the combined wing package. The certification work is fold-system failure modes (777X-style special conditions, my inference) and CS 25.302 interaction of systems and structures for the load-alleviation laws. Airbus's eXtra Performance Wing (semi-aeroelastic hinged tips, gust sensors, pop-up spoilers) is due to fly on a one-third-scale Citation in late 2026 but has published no results ([Aviación al Día](https://aviacionaldia.com/en/2026/07/airbus-begins-flight-testing-high-aspect-ratio-wings-for-its-next-generation-narrowbody-aircraft.html)). It is the most likely route to combining the gust-relief hinge and the ground fold.

Laminar flow is useful at Mach 0.85, but only in hybrid form or on surfaces with little sweep. Airbus's BLADE natural-laminar wing targeted **up to 8% drag and ~4.5% fuel at Mach 0.75** ([AviTrader](https://avitrader.com/2017/09/26/european-laminar-flow-research-takes-new-step-with-airbus-blade-flight-lab/)), but no measured fuel result was ever published. Its design Mach also conflicts with your cruise requirement, because wing sweep at Mach 0.85 destabilises natural laminar flow. Hybrid laminar flow control (leading-edge suction) is already in service on the 787-9 fin and tailplane, although Boeing has declined to give a fuel figure ([AviationPros](https://www.aviationpros.com/aircraft/commercial-airline/news/11151882/boeings-20-foot-longer-787-9-could-take-flight-today)). A 2025 Aeronautical Journal assessment of wing-integrated HLFC on a long-haul aircraft modelled **more than 3% on the design mission and 1.6–2.5% on realistic routes** ([Aeronautical Journal](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/040885D53933FFB5DDF6B0B2F4EC140E/S0001924025000260a.pdf/performance-and-economic-assessment-of-a-wing-integrated-hybrid-laminar-flow-control-system.pdf)). I estimate the empennage and nacelle applications at TRL 6–7 and the wing at TRL 5. The penalties are suction-system weight, perforated-skin manufacturing cost, and sensitivity to insects and contamination. Riblet film is the cheapest gain. Lufthansa Technik and BASF's AeroSHARK is EASA-approved on the 777 and was rolled out across the SWISS 777 fleet on simulations predicting **"just over 1%"** ([Lufthansa Technik](https://newsroom.lufthansa-technik.com/aeroshark-successfully-rolled-out-to-the-entire-swiss-boeing-777-fleet/)). However, every published figure, including SWISS's 2,200 t of kerosene saved in 2023, is extrapolated from that estimate rather than measured independently.

Two systems design choices also act in cruise. The A350's differential flap setting is in service. A related Airbus-linked paper claims variable camber gives **up to 2% drag reduction at high gross weight** and saves about half a tonne of wing-box weight ([EU TRIMIS](https://trimis.ec.europa.eu/system/files/project/documents/20121030_122633_15453_Advanced_High_Lift_System_Architecture_with_Distributed_Electrical_Flap_Actuation.pdf); [Liebherr](https://www.liebherr.com/en/fra/specials/aer-newsletters/electronics/2018-12_part-1/part-1.html)). If your 2020 competitor is an A350 or 787, which already have trailing-edge camber control, this is parity rather than a gain. A no-bleed electrical architecture is reported to give the 787 **about 3% better fuel burn**, but the source is secondary and no Boeing primary figure was found ([PlaneFYI](https://planefyi.com/systems/no-bleed-architecture/)). The A350 retained bleed air, so against an A350 baseline this is a genuine 1–3% (estimate), partly overlapping with engine-cycle gains. Cruise CG control through a trim tank, as on every A330 ([UBI thesis](https://ubibliorum.ubi.pt/bitstreams/1ed70aef-aa33-4dc2-99b2-5482b382daf6/download)), or through relaxed-stability fly-by-wire is worth about 0.5–1.5% by the researchers' estimate. Only a weakly sourced site quantifies it.

The radical configurations are poor 2040 choices, for reasons specific to this brief. NASA and Boeing claim **up to 30% fuel reduction** for the transonic truss-braced wing, but that figure includes engine and other technology assumptions. The braced wing's own effect in SUGAR was about **8% against a cantilever equivalent**, and the design cruise was Mach 0.75–0.80 ([ICAS 2018](https://www.icas.org/icas_archive/ICAS2018/data/papers/ICAS2018_0597_paper.pdf); [NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001025/downloads/20170001025.pdf)). The X-66A flight demonstrator was **paused on 24 April 2025** ([AvWeb](https://avweb.com/aviation-news/boeing-nasa-pause-x-66a-flight-program)). Blended wing bodies carry headline claims of 20% (Airbus MAVERIC, same engines) to 50% (JetZero, including engine assumptions) ([IMechE](https://www.imeche.org/news/news-article/airbus-blended-wing-plane-could-cut-fuel-consumption-by-20); [CompositesWorld](https://www.compositesworld.com/news/jetzero-closes-100-million-financing-facility-to-advance-z4-program)). JetZero's full-scale demonstrator is not due to fly until 2027, and CS 25.803 evacuation (90 s using half the exits) has only been shown in simulation. Researchers question whether existing exits can serve four or five aisles ([The Engineer](https://www.theengineer.co.uk/simulated-safety/); [TAR 2026](https://reference-global.com/article/10.2478/tar-2026-0006)). ICAO's LTAG assessment concluded that advanced tube-and-wing aircraft have "clear potential" and that unconventional configurations make only an "incremental contribution" within the 2050 horizon ([ICAO HLM-LTAG](https://www.icao.int:443/Meetings/HLM-LTAG/Documents/HLM.LTAG.WP.04.en.pdf)). That is the strongest single citation for keeping your aircraft tube-and-wing.

## Take-off and landing systems buy maintenance savings, not fuel

The terminal phases are a small share of a 6.5 h trip, so their fuel savings are tiny, but several have real cost value. EUROCONTROL estimates continuous descent saves **35 kg per arrival on average, 46 kg where CDO is not already flown** ([EUROCONTROL](https://www.eurocontrol.int/concept/continuous-climb-and-descent-operations)). That is about 0.1% of trip fuel (estimate), although widebody savings are probably higher than the network average. A reduced-flap landing saves 7–10 kg on an A320 ([Open Airlines](https://blog.openairlines.com/what-you-need-to-know-about-reduced-landing-flaps)). Airbus warns that combining CONF 3 with idle reverse and medium or high autobrake can significantly increase carbon brake oxidation ([Airbus Safety First](https://flightsafety.airbus.com/2019/11/22/smart-adoption-of-fuel-economy-initiatives/)), which shows that fuel and maintenance savings can work against each other.

Derated take-off is a cost measure that slightly increases fuel burn. Boeing Flight Operations states that full rated thrust saves fuel but raises engine maintenance cost ([Boeing/ICAO 2006](https://www2023.icao.int/Meetings/EnvironmentalWorkshops/Documents/ICAO-TransportCanada-2006/Anderson_ops.pdf)). Derates of 5–10% can raise EGT margin by up to 36 degrees ([US Patent 9816897](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9816897)), and EGT margin deterioration drives most engine removals. The design lever is take-off L/D. Aviation Partners claims winglets allow **about 3% lower take-off thrust** ([APB](https://aviationpartnersboeing.com/winglets_remc.php)). A high-span wing should therefore be argued as a maintenance-cost benefit too, which matters for a geared engine whose shop-visit cost is uncertain.

Brake technology adds weight and maintenance benefits. Safran's 787 electric brakes save **64 kg on the 787-8 and 111 kg on the 787-9**, remove the hydraulic lines, and allow dispatch with one actuator inoperative ([Safran](https://www.safran-group.com/products-services/boeing-787-dreamliner-electric-brake)). That is very roughly 0.1–0.2% of trip fuel through the weight rule of thumb (estimate). Airbus's Brake-to-Vacate, available on the A350, claimed **up to 25% longer brake life** and lower runway occupancy ([AIN](https://oldain.ainonline.com/aviation-news/aviation-international-news/2009-06-30/pilot-report-airbus-a380); [SKYbrary](https://skybrary.aero/articles/brake-vacate-btv)). Both are TRL 9 and cheap to specify. No regenerative braking system has been found on any airliner.

The ≤150 kt approach-speed requirement looks comfortable for this aircraft. The A350-900's Vapp at MLW is about 140 kt, although that figure comes from a secondary source citing the Airbus characteristics document and needs checking ([Infinite Flight community](https://community.infiniteflight.com/t/final-approach-speeds-for-aircraft/528955)). A high-aspect-ratio wing with lower span loading helps further. Clean Sky's morphing single-slotted flap and gapless droop nose aim to match conventional CLmax with less noise and complexity ([SPIE](https://electronicimaging.spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/10166/101660L/Experimental-validation-of-a-true-scale-morphing-flap-for-large/10.1117/12.2259878.full); [Fraunhofer LBF](https://www.lbf.fraunhofer.de/content/dam/lbf/de/documents/veranstaltungen/Fraunhofer%20Droop%20Nose.pdf)). I estimate their TRL at 4–6, so they are plausible but not certain for 2040. Their case rests on part count and noise, not fuel. Steep approach is likewise a noise and airport-access measure. The A318 needed spoilers used as airbrakes, a cockpit selector and a demonstrated 7.5° capability for 5.5° approval ([FlightGlobal](https://www.flightglobal.com/heading-for-the-square-mile/67515.article)), while 3.2° trials gave only **0.5 dBA average** noise reduction ([Luton DCO submission](https://infrastructure.planninginspectorate.gov.uk/wp-content/ipc/uploads/projects/TR020001/TR020001-002476-LADACAN-Post-hearing-submission-Slightly-Steeper-Approach-Study-Report-2021.pdf)). The GABRIEL maglev take-off concept (EU FP7, €3.3 m) claimed gear deletion and smaller engines but published no quantified result, and it would require every diversion airport to be equipped ([CORDIS](https://www.cordis.europa.eu/project/id/284884)). Exclude it.

Electric taxiing was covered earlier and only needs restating briefly. Towing on departure is the preferred route, and on-board taxi motors do not pay back their weight over a 6.5 h sector. As a 2026 data point, an all-electric TaxiBot has towed easyJet A320neos at Schiphol since April 2026 with a claimed **~95 kg fuel per cycle**, but a widebody version is still in development ([Schiphol](https://www.schiphol.nl/en/sustainability/blog/electric-taxibot-starts-operating-at-schiphol)). The design requirement is towbar and tow-vehicle compatibility, not motors.

## Operations add 2–4 points that the aircraft cannot guarantee

Cruise operations are additive with airframe and engine gains, because they cut distance, time or weight rather than SFC. However, airlines and air navigation service providers control them, so they cannot be credited in an aircraft requirement. Airspace inefficiency is the biggest pool: European gate-to-gate inefficiency attributable to air navigation is **about 6% of unimpeded fuel burn, with an ambition of 2.3% by 2035** ([ICAO Stocktaking 2020](https://www.icao.int:443/Meetings/Stocktaking2020/Documents/ICAO%20Stocktaking%202020%20-%202.3.6%20-%20Eric%20Perrin.pdf)). EUROCONTROL puts free-route savings at roughly 2–3% depending on the airspace ([EUROCONTROL via ICAO](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/EURNAT/Documents/EUR%20and%20Nat%20Docs/Environment/2019%20Regional%20Seminar%20on%20Environment/EUROCONTROL-Operational-mitigations_final.pdf)). Much of that pool will be captured by 2040 regardless of aircraft type, which reduces any advantage over the 2020 competitor flying in the same airspace. The aircraft's contribution is a 4D-capable FMS with datalink, as flown in Airbus's i4D trials ([Aviation Today](https://www.aviationtoday.com/2014/03/20/airbus-conducts-sesar-i4d-flight-trials/)).

EASA's 2022 fuel schemes allow **3% rather than 5% contingency fuel** with an en-route alternate, or statistical contingency, provided each aircraft has fuel-consumption monitoring ([Honeywell](https://www.honeywellaerospace.com/us/en/about-us/news/2022/06/navigating-understanding-easa-latest-fuel-energy-requirements)). Using the rule that about 3.5% of carried mass is burned per hour ([OpenAirlines](https://blog.openairlines.com/how-to-use-the-cost-of-weight-to-be-more-fuel-efficient)), that is worth about 0.4% (estimate). Fitting monitoring as standard is cheap and makes the saving available to operators.

Wake-energy retrieval is the most interesting operational idea and the least certain. In the 2021 fello'fly demonstration, the follower A350 saved **"over 5% (around 2 tonnes)"** while flying in formation for about 80% of the flight ([Skies Magazine](https://skiesmag.com/?p=335039)). The 2025 GEESE trials, however, validated only the rendezvous, with full vertical separation, and the partners are still seeking authority support to certify the concept ([Airbus](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/stories/2026-02-geese-and-atm-wake-energy-retrieval-collaboration-achieves-new-milestones)). Only the follower benefits, so a pair averages about 2.5%. Pairing is also easiest on dense oceanic tracks rather than 3,000 NM continental sectors, so the realistic fleet average is about 0.5–1.5% (estimate). WER-ready autopilot laws and wake-fatigue substantiation are cheaper to include at type design than to retrofit. Still, no ICAO separation standard exists, so it is an option to protect, not a benefit to count.

Two further points affect the cost argument. Contrail avoidance is a fuel cost rather than a saving: the 2023 Google and American Airlines trial measured about 2% extra fuel on the flights that deviated ([Energy Monitor](https://www.energymonitor.ai/newsletters/ai-cuts-climate-warming-contrails-of-planes-by-54-google-study)), although a 2025 trial of about 2,400 flights found no statistically significant fuel difference ([AP via WTOP](https://wtop.com/europe/2026/03/american-airlines-and-google-say-ai-helped-airplanes-reduce-contrails-that-trap-heat/)). 100% SAF already cut contrail ice crystals by **56% per kg of fuel** in DLR's ECLIF3 A350 measurements ([DLR](https://www.dlr.de/en/latest/news/2024/flying-using-100-percent-sustainable-aviation-fuel-significantly-reduces-non-carbon-dioxide-emissions)). Cost index is the second point. SAF raises fuel cost relative to time cost, so the optimum cost index falls towards long-range cruise, which is slower. If 2040 operators fly slower anyway, a design Mach of about 0.82 rather than 0.85 would recover part of the laminar-flow and wing benefit lost to compressibility. No SAF-specific cost-index study was found, so this is reasoning from the definition of cost index, but it is the strongest argument for challenging the Mach requirement at SRR.

The honest conclusion for a requirements argument is that a realistic, certifiable design package reaches about 20%, about two-thirds of the 29% target. Operations bring the total to about 22–23%, leaving roughly 6–7 points. The design package only reaches about 28% if the engine delivers 13%, the wing 10% and laminar flow 3% together. That depends on everything going right, and the wing and engine figures are optimistic because they are not independent: a lighter engine shrinks the wing, but a larger fan adds gear weight. Two levers outside this report's scope could credibly close the remainder, and both belong in your SRR trade studies. The first is relaxing cruise Mach to about 0.82, which the SAF-era cost index supports and which recovers laminar and wing benefits. The second is weight and seat-density measures, where each tonne of empty weight removed is worth about 0.5–0.7% of fuel (estimate from the 3.5%/h rule). The cost argument can also lean on non-fuel savings: derate-friendly take-off performance, electric brakes and BTV lower maintenance cost, and composite-wing manufacturing improvements may lower build cost. These offset part of the SAF premium without appearing in fuel energy per seat. No source in this research quantifies their size.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the problem. The 29% target is not a hunt for one breakthrough. The breakthroughs that promise it alone (open fan, truss-braced wing, BWB) are specifically disqualified by Mach 0.85, the 300-seat thrust class or the 2040 certification timeline. What remains is a set of mature or near-mature items, each with a modest and often only manufacturer-claimed benefit. Their product falls a few points short, and the shortfall should be stated openly at SRR rather than hidden in optimistic assumptions. The most valuable requirement your team can write may therefore be the trade itself: a study of design Mach 0.82 against 0.85 under SAF-era cost index, with the folding-span wing, geared engine, HLFC and no-bleed systems as firm requirements and wake-energy retrieval as a protected provision.

The evidence base also has a weakness to declare. Almost no headline figure in this field has been measured independently: UltraFan's 10%, AeroSHARK's 1%, HLFC's 787 benefit and the no-bleed 3% are all targets, simulations or secondary reports. The only clean flight measurements found are the fello'fly follower saving and the ECLIF3 contrail data. A rigorous SRR should carry each technology with a confidence band and a named baseline. If the competitor is an A350-900, the engine and systems gains shrink and the gap widens. If it is an A330neo or 787-8, the package comes closer to 29% on paper.
